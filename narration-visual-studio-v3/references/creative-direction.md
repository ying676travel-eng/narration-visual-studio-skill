# Creative direction

For a complete talk, plan its narrative and page breaks with [deck-storytelling.md](deck-storytelling.md) first. Use this guide after reading the full timed transcript and before writing scene HTML. The goal is to help the viewer understand the explanation. A scene is a complete thought, not a subtitle row.

## Start with the idea

For each scene, write one plain sentence: “By the end of this frame, the viewer should understand ___.” Then name the relationship that carries the idea:

- **Claim:** one clear statement or conclusion.
- **Comparison:** what changes between two states.
- **Cause and effect:** what action leads to what result.
- **Sequence:** what happens first, next, and last.
- **System:** how parts relate to a whole or to one another.
- **Evidence:** what a source image, supplied figure, or observed example proves.
- **Metaphor:** a concrete visual stand-in for an abstract idea.

If the sentence is hard to write, the scene is not ready to design. Re-read the surrounding cues and join sentences that explain the same thought. Do not split merely because a subtitle line ended.

Use [composition-system.md](composition-system.md) for the concrete alignment, grouping, Chinese type and safe-area rules. Plan one leading focal point, then a secondary reading step; do not scatter equally weighted explanations.

## Choose the visual form

Choose what the audience should see change, then choose a composition that makes that change easy to follow. A statement may need scale and quiet space. A comparison may need two simultaneous states. A process may need a route that grows in speaking order. A system may need an original diagram with clear connections. Evidence should use the real supplied source; when no source exists, say so instead of inventing a screenshot or statistic.

Treat each pack as a visual voice, not a page template. Cartesian brings a fine-line, warm-stone editorial voice; Code Editorial brings warm paper, literary display, and a deliberate technical surface; BlockFrame brings hard geometry, saturated pastel planes, and blunt contrast. Keep the voice consistent while changing the composition to match the meaning. A style pack constrains color, type, geometry, and emphasis; it does not dictate where every object goes.

Before coding, decide:

1. Which visual relationship carries this scene?
2. What must be visible first, and what may appear later?
3. Does the idea need a drawn diagram, or can type and space explain it more clearly?
4. Which words are short on-screen labels, rather than a transcript printed on the frame?
5. Which items are supplied evidence, and which are clearly illustrative drawings?
6. How does this frame hand the viewer into the next scene?

## Keep facts traceable

Carry facts and timing from the source cues. Do not add data, product details, interface screenshots, or causal claims to make a composition look fuller. Mark illustrative pseudo-code and abstract diagrams as illustrative when a viewer could mistake them for real product evidence. If the source is ambiguous, keep the ambiguity visible in the scene plan rather than resolving it by design.

## Check the composition

Ask where the eye lands first, where it goes second, and which visual change clarifies the narration. Delete any decorative element that has no job in that reading path. Compare the scene with its neighbors: they should belong to the same style, yet should not look like the same arrangement with different words.
